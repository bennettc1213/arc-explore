Time Is of the Essence: Experimental Evidence on the Effects of Job Application Timing ∗  Sai Zhang †  October 21, 2026  [Update regularly, click for the latest version]  Abstract  This paper studies the role of job application timing in recruitment. Repurposing the correspondence experiment of Kline, Rose and Walters (2022), I show that, holding vacancy and resume content fixed, first-half experimental applications have a contact rate 1.16 percentage points higher, equivalent to 57 percent of the Black-White contact gap in the same setting. Each day earlier raises contact probability by 0.40 percentage points.   The key mechanism is that firms often begin contacting while applications are still being received. Using randomized resume attributes, I instrument whether contact has already begun with the highest predicted contact probability among pre- viously submitted applications. Stronger prior applications trigger earlier initiation, and IV estimates confirm a post-initiation contact penalty.   The timing gradient is steeper in tighter labor markets. A sequential model separates applicant arrival rates from the shadow cost of delay and quantifies firms’ tradeoff between earlier contact and the screening-value gains from waiting. Conditional on the firm contacting an experimental application, the highest-ranked application has not yet arrived in 51.6 percent of cases, yielding a 6.6 percent loss relative to the contact-initiating appli- cation’s expected screening value. Together, the results provide the first large-scale causal evidence that firms’ sequential contact decisions make queue position conse- quential within vacancies, revealing an employer-side margin through which labor market conditions shape who is considered.  ∗ I am indebted to Vittorio Bassi, Simon Quach and Pablo Kurlat for their invaluable guidance and support. 1   Introduction  Job applications arrive over time, but firms must decide when to stop waiting and begin contacting candidates. Waiting expands the applicant pool and may reveal a better match, while acting sooner shortens vacancy delay and can secure candi- dates before they become unavailable. The timing of contact is therefore part of the firm’s recruiting policy: employers can respond to scarce applicants not only through wages, amenities, or recruiting effort, but by moving faster. Workers face the mirror image of this problem. Additional preparation may improve the tar- geting and content of an application, but it can also move the applicant behind the firm’s contact decision. When firms act before the pool is complete, otherwise comparable applicants can receive different consideration simply because they ar- rive at different points in the vacancy’s recruiting process. Application timing can therefore shape both who receives consideration and the quality of the pool from which firms ultimately select. Recent work shows that workers’ choices on when to search, apply, and accept offers can have persistent consequences for their employment outcomes (Bandiera, Jalal and Roussille, 2025; Cortés et al., 2023), while large-scale application data doc- ument how quickly workers respond to vacancies in modern hiring markets (Davis and de la Parra, 2024). This paper studies the complementary firm-side margin: how sequential screening makes relative application timing consequential. Iden- tifying this margin is difficult because an application timestamp is endogenous to job seekers’ information, motivation, constraints, and application quality, while firms’ internal recruiting stage and organic applicant pool are usually unobserved. Aggregate data, in turn, compress these within-vacancy dynamics into vacancy durations, job-filling hazards, or matching-efficiency residuals. To causally identify the application timing effect in the recruiting process, I repurpose the large-scale correspondence experiment of Kline, Rose and Walters (2022).   The scale of the experiment is unusual: it generated 83,643 applications to 11,114 entry-level vacancies at 108 major U.S. firms.   Each vacancy received up to eight fictitious applications with randomized characteristics, submitted in four pairs spaced one to two days apart, and the dates of employer contacts were recorded. About 24 percent of these applications received employer contact within 30 days, consistent with firms viewing the experimental applicants as credible can- 1

didates. The original experiment was designed to measure discrimination. I use its controlled arrival sequence for a different purpose: randomized applications to the same vacancy reveal how employer contact decisions evolve as the applicant pool accumulates. In this sense, the applications serve as experimental tracers of the vacancy’s recruiting process. Exploiting this design, I identify the causal effect of relative arrival time and trace how contact initiation changes the consideration of subsequently arriving applications.   To quantify the firm tradeoff underlying these effects, I then develop and estimate a sequential contact-initiation model and use it to conduct counterfactuals. To my knowledge, this is the first large-scale ex- perimental study on the role of application timing in firms’ recruiting process and its consequences for applicants. 1  I first establish a sizable   early application advantage . Applications submitted in the first half of the experimental sequence are 1.16 percentage points more likely to receive employer contact within 30 days. This is an increase of roughly 5 percent over the average contact rate. Each additional day before the final experimental submission raises contact probability by 0.40 percentage points, and the timing profile is approximately monotone over the experimental window.   These esti- mates are stable across alternative timing definitions, sample restrictions, wave- specific submission protocols, and calendar controls. As a scale benchmark, the first-half effect is about 57 percent of the Black-White contact gap in the same ex- periment. The timing effect raises the central mechanism question: why should arrival timing matter when resume content is randomized? Two families of mechanisms could explain this pattern: direct-timing mechanisms, in which earlier submissions receive mechanical priority or signal motivation, and pool-based mechanisms, in which timing matters through the set of applications available when firms act. The evidence points to a threshold-triggered transition into the contact phase: firms be- gin contacting once the best application received so far is sufficiently attractive to justify proceeding rather than waiting for more applications. The highest screening value among previously submitted experimen- tal applications predicts both entry into the contact phase and earlier initiation, whereas their mean screening value does not. Entry into the contact phase substantially changes the prospects of later appli- cants. Contacts occur quickly: conditional on contact within 30 days, 52 percent of contacts occur within three days after submission and 68 percent within six days. Nearly half of vacancies with at least one contacted experimental application con- tinue to receive submissions after the first observed contact. An event-study com- parison, controlling flexibly for submission order, shows a sharp decline in contact probability for applications arriving after contact initiation. Constructed contact histories imply a similar pattern: the contact rate is about 63 percent lower after at least one earlier experimental contact. Because resume attributes are random- ized across submission positions, the best-so-far screening value provides an in- strument for whether a later application arrives after contact initiation.   The IV estimates support a negative post-initiation effect on subsequent contact. Additional tests weigh against alternative mechanisms. The early advantage disappears when contact begins only after the full experimental sequence has ar- rived, weighing against a direct timing premium whether mechanical or signaling- based. The quality of future applications does not predict whether earlier appli- cations are contacted, weighing against full-pool comparison. Later submissions are contacted later, making pure batch review difficult to reconcile with the bundle of evidence. Together, these results support a sequential threshold mechanism in which firms begin contacting before the experimental pool is complete and entry into the contact phase lowers the consideration of later applicants. If contact initiation reflects the value and cost of waiting, the strength of the timing margin should vary with recruiting conditions. It does. The daily penalty for arriving later is steeper in tighter state-month labor markets, both for overall contact and for the probability of being the experimental contact initiator.   This evidence is descriptive rather than a causal estimate of tightness, but it provides a disciplined micro-to-macro fact: when workers are scarce relative to vacancies, firms move through applicant queues more quickly and the return to reaching a vacancy before contact initiation rises. The model em- beds the experimental applications in a broader applicant flow and separates two forces that the reduced form conflates. The first is the effective arrival intensity,   λ , which governs how quickly additional applicant opportunities arrive. Following directed-search models, this arrival environment depends on market congestion and on a shifter,   ξ , that summarizes hiring and separation conditions not captured by tightness alone (Birinci, See and Wee, 2025).   The second is the shadow cost of delaying contact,   κ , which can reflect vacancy delay, the risk that candidates become unavailable, and organizational pressure to move the recruiting process forward. The firm chooses when to initiate contact by comparing this cost with the expected screening-value gain from waiting for additional applications. The esti- mated model reproduces the front-loaded timing of experimental contact initiation and its variation with tightness. I then use thze model to quantify firms’ tradeoff between recruiting speed and the screening-value gains from waiting. Conditional on the firm contacting an ex- perimental application, the highest-screening-value experimental application has not yet arrived at contact initiation in 51.6 percent of cases. The screening-value gap between the highest-valued experimental application overall and the highest- valued one available at initiation averages 6.6 percent of the screening value of the initiated application. A deliberately conservative back-of-the-envelope conversion places this baseline timing loss at approximately $390-$781 per vacancy-year. 2   The counterfactuals trace out a speed-loss frontier as firms adjust contact timing to changes in delay costs and applicant arrival conditions. Halving the shadow cost of delay leads firms to wait longer and lowers the relative timing loss from 6.6 to 6.2 percent; doubling it induces earlier contact and raises the loss to 7.1 percent. Timing losses also rise with labor-market tightness. Taken together, this paper identifies a structural timing friction within a va- cancy’s matching process. The friction does not require a departure from firm pri- vate optimization: it arises because applicant arrivals are sequential and waiting is costly. Firms initiate contact when the expected screening-value gain from waiting for additional applications no longer exceeds the shadow cost of delay.   The ex- perimental design makes this tradeoff observable and quantifiable. Heterogeneity in delay costs and arrival environments generates different contact-initiation poli- cies, which in turn create a private return to queue position for applicants and a potential arms race in monitoring and submission speed. At the aggregate level, this employer-side timing margin offers a microeconomic channel through which labor-market conditions affect matching efficiency by changing when firms act on their applicant pools. More accurate screening can improve the ranking of applica- tions already received, but it need not eliminate this friction as long as applications arrive sequentially and firms compete on time. Even if application submission and initial screening become nearly instantaneous, the same timing logic may reappear in interviews, offers, or other downstream stages. Within-vacancy timing is there- fore an economic margin of match formation, not merely an administrative feature of recruiting.  Contribution to the literature.   This paper first contributes to a growing literature on the timing and dynamics of worker search. Recent work shows that workers’ choices on when to search can have persistent employment consequences and con- tribute to gender gaps (Bandiera, Jalal and Roussille, 2025). Related evidence stud- ies the timing of offer acceptance and early-career outcomes (Cortés et al., 2023). Large-scale application data document how quickly workers direct applications across vacancies (Davis and de la Parra, 2024), while field experiments show that application costs and dynamic selection shape the composition of applicant pools (Abebe, Caria and Ortiz-Ospina, 2021). This literature establishes that   when workers search, apply and accept offers   matters. I identify a complementary firm-side mech- anism: holding application content fixed, relative arrival time changes employer consideration because firms act on an evolving applicant queue. The design there- fore isolates the causal return to queue position from endogenous worker selection and identifies the firm behavior that produces this return. The original experiment was designed to study discrimination among large U.S. employers. I use a different feature of the design: each sampled vacancy received multiple randomized applications submitted over several days. I refer to these applications as experimental tracers because their staggered ar- rival and observed contact outcomes reveal how the employer’s recruiting process evolves as applications accumulate within a vacancy. This section summarizes the features of the experiment that are needed for the analysis.   I first describe the experimental setting, then discuss how the timing of the experimental applications maps into a vacancy’s recruiting window, and finally introduce the complementary labor-market data used later in the paper. 2.1   Experimental Setting  The experiment sampled large U.S. employers from the 2018 Fortune 500 list. Fol- lowing Kline, Rose and Walters (2022), the sampling frame was restricted to com- panies with distinct brands, proprietary hiring portals, broad geographic reach, and a sufficient volume of entry-level vacancies to support the audit design. The resulting sample includes 108 firms across a wide range of industries, collectively employing roughly 15 million workers in 2020. Vacancies were sampled over five waves from October 2019 to April 2021. For each employer, the original experiment targeted 125 entry-level vacancies, with 25 vacancies per wave. A majority of firms (72) participated in all waves. Some firms missed Wave 1 because of COVID-19 disruptions, while others were excluded from later waves because of hiring portal changes and technological barriers. For each employer, sampled vacancies corresponded to establishments in counties not pre- viously sampled for that employer. Firms entered the sampling queue in random- ized order within each wave, and vacancies were sampled from the firms’ own hiring portals. The vacancies were entry-level positions that did not require a bachelor’s or advanced degree, substantial prior experience, or a specialized license. For each sampled vacancy, the experiment submitted up to eight applications with random- ized resume characteristics. Applications were sent in four pairs. Within each pair, one application carried a distinctively Black name and the other a distinctively White name. Gender, age, education, employment history, and other resume char- acteristics were randomized as in Kline, Rose and Walters (2022). Some vacancies stopped accepting applications before all eight submissions were completed. The final sample contains 83,643 applications to 11,114 vacancies, and 88.4% of vacan- cies received the full set of eight experimental applications. The outcome is employer contact within 30 days of submission. Each fictitious applicant had a unique phone number and email address, which were monitored to record employer callbacks, interview requests, or other positive employer re- sponses. The average contact rate is about 24%, making the setting well suited to studying heterogeneity in employer contact behavior. 4  4 As in most correspondence experiments, the outcome is employer contact rather than inter- views, offers, hires, or wages. 2.2   Experimental Timing within a Vacancy  The timing of the experimental submissions is central to this paper. Kline, Rose and Walters (2022) scraped firms’ hiring portals daily and, within each wave, sam- pled the most recent job posting in a new county for each firm, randomizing ties. Applications were then submitted over a short window.   In Wave 1, pairs were typically sent every other day; beginning in Wave 2, when most applications were submitted automatically, one pair was typically sent per day. Overall, 94% of ap- plications were submitted within eight days of the first experimental application to the vacancy. This design places the experimental submissions within an active application window and relatively close to the posting’s appearance on the employer portal. At the same time, the experiment does not observe the exact vacancy posting date, the full organic applicant pool, or the employer’s recruiting stage when the first experimental application arrives. The estimand in this paper is therefore the ef- fect of arriving earlier   within the experimental submission window , not the return to applying immediately after a vacancy is first posted. 5  This use of the experiment differs from the usual correspondence experiment design, which focuses on average differences in contact rates across randomized applicant characteristics.   Here, the repeated, timed applications trace how em- ployer contact decisions change as the experimental sequence unfolds, revealing within-vacancy screening dynamics that are usually unobserved.  2.3   Complementary Market Data  To study how the application timing gradient varies with recruiting conditions and to parameterize the recruiting environment in the model, I merge experimen- tal vacancies to state-month labor-market indicators. The main market condition measure is local labor market tightness from the Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS), defined as vacancies per unemployed worker in the state of the vacancy and the month in which the first experimental application to that vacancy is submitted. In the full sample, applications in the first half of the experimental sequence are 1.16 percentage points more likely to receive contact, and each additional day before the final experimental submission raises contact probability by 0.40 percentage points. The estimates are nearly iden- tical in the complete eight-application sample. Appendix Section A.2 reports ro- bustness to alternative timing definitions, designed pair order, wave-specific sub- mission protocols, incomplete-vacancy restrictions, and calendar controls. These results establish the timing fact that motivates the rest of the paper, but they do not explain why relative arrival time matters. Section 4 therefore turns to employer-side recruiting dynamics and asks whether the advantage arises because firms enter the contact phase before later applications arrive. Due to data limitations, I leave the interpretation of these group-specific dy- namics as an important direction for future work. A large literature in directed and competitive search emphasizes that firms and workers interact through both matching probabilities and queues (Wolthoff, 2018; 28

Wright et al., 2021; Carrillo-Tudela, Gartner and Kaas, 2023): recruiting policies determine not only whether a firm fills a vacancy, but also how quickly it does so and how selective it can be. In this sense, the timing margin is part of firms’ re- cruiting technology. When labor markets are tight, firms face a thinner queue of applications and greater job-filling pressure. The finding in Table 6 that the daily timing gradient is steeper in tighter state-month labor markets is therefore consis- tent with a recruiting-pressure interpretation: recruiters have stronger incentives to initiate contact quickly, and applications arriving after contact initiation are pe- nalized more sharply. I do not interpret this heterogeneity as causal evidence that tightness changes firm behavior in isolation. Rather, I use it as a disciplined de- scriptive pattern that the model should rationalize. Tables A.20 and A.21 report analogous specifications using additional state- month labor-market series from JOLTS: hires rate, quits rate, layoffs and discharges rate, and job-opening rate. These series capture distinct dimensions of the recruit- ing environment that may affect the opportunity cost of delaying contact.   The estimates point in the same general direction for contact initiation, but they are noisier for the overall contact outcome.   Because these aggregate indicators are correlated and not randomly assigned, I report them both separately and jointly as descriptive robustness exercises rather than as separate causal tests.   The ad- ditional JOLTS series are not used in the main reduced-form argument. Instead, they inform the macro-state specification in the model, where I combine them into a parsimonious recruiting-state shifter that allows the contact-initiation policy to vary with broader labor-market conditions not captured by tightness alone. Taken together, the heterogeneity evidence connects the within-vacancy mech- anism of contact initiation to broader labor-market conditions. The early applica- tion advantage is more pronounced when vacancy-filling pressure is higher. The model is intentionally partial equilibrium.   It focuses on the recruiting subgame conditional on a posted vacancy and a given recruiting environment, abstracting from the complete hiring pipeline and the general-equilibrium feed- back from one firm’s contact timing. This scope matches the experimental variation: the design observes how tracer appli- cations are processed within a vacancy, but not the equilibrium process that gen- erated the vacancy or the applicant pool. The reduced-form tightness gradients and the estimated recruiting-state shifter   ξ j   sug- gest that this timing margin varies with labor-market conditions. These results pro- vide a way to microfound the cyclicality of matching efficiency through changes in employer-side screening and contact timing.   Aggregate search models often summarize variation not explained by unemployment and vacancies as match- ing efficiency (Barnichon and Figura, 2015), and micro evidence shows that firms adjust recruiting intensity and hiring standards over the cycle (Davis, Faberman and Haltiwanger, 2013; Carrillo-Tudela, Gartner and Kaas, 2023). The model es- timates a micro analogue of this margin at an earlier stage of the hiring process: how quickly vacancies move from receiving applications to initiating contact. Finally, the framework clarifies how new screening technologies may change, but not necessarily eliminate, timing frictions. More efficient screening may reduce the cost of ranking applications that have already arrived and may compress the initial screening stage. But as long as applications arrive sequentially and firms make decisions before later candidates appear, the queue-position wedge remains. In the extreme case in which job seekers also adopt technology that submits ap- plications immediately after posting, the contact-stage timing game may unravel toward the posting date. Even then, as long as hiring remains sequential, similar timing wedges can reappear later in the pipeline: in interview scheduling, offer timing, candidate follow-up, or final selection.   The model therefore provides a framework for studying how the timing of employer decisions shapes who is con- sidered as recruiting technology and labor-market conditions change.  7   Conclusion  This paper studies how application timing affects employer consideration through firms’ sequential screening. I use experimentally timed resume submissions to trace employer-side screening within vacancies, holding resume content fixed while varying when applications arrive. The central empirical finding is a causal early application advantage. Earlier experimental submissions are more likely to receive employer contact, and the timing gradient is larger in tighter local labor markets. The mechanism evidence identifies contact initiation as the key change in the recruiting process. Contacts occur quickly, applications submitted after a vacancy enters the contact phase face lower contact probabilities, and contact initiation responds to the highest- screening-value application observed so far. In contrast, the evidence does not sup- port explanations based primarily on applicant selection, firms waiting to compare the full experimental pool, or a direct premium on early submission, for example, because an early timestamp signals motivation. To organize these facts, I develop a partial-equilibrium model of contact initia- tion. Firms choose when to enter the contact phase by solving an optimal stopping problem, trading off the option value of waiting for additional applications against a shadow cost of delay. The model separates the rate at which additional appli- cant opportunities arrive from the shadow cost of delay and maps experimental application outcomes into a contact-initiation policy. This structure allows me to quantify the screening-value cost of faster initiation while remaining explicit about the object being identified: a contact-initiation policy on the experimental margin, not the full hiring technology. The counterfactuals show that the timing structure of recruiting creates a mean- ingful speed-quality tradeoff. Faster contact initiation raises the probability that the highest-screening-value experimental application has not yet arrived, and it increases the screening-value loss associated with moving before later applications enter the pool. The speed-loss frontier quantifies the screening value forgone when privately optimizing firms move faster in response to delay costs and applicant arrival conditions. At a given initiation date, better ranking among applications already in the pool cannot eliminate the timing loss from higher-screening-value applications that arrive later. This distinction is important: recruiting frictions arise not only from imperfect screening, but also from the timing structure of the recruit- 46

PAGE 48
ing process itself. The results have important implications for firms, applicants, and aggregate matching. For firms, the relevant margin is a choice over recruiting speed: mov- ing quickly may be optimal when vacancies are costly to leave open, but it also reduces the option value of later applications. For applicants, the timing premium creates a private return to speed that can incentivize job seekers to monitor post- ings more frequently, apply quickly, or compress preparation time, even when these investments do not improve match quality.   Therefore, the private return to early application may exceed its social return. The experiment does not mea- sure worker-side constraints directly, but it shows that the labor market rewards the application speed margin on which such constraints could matter for both effi- ciency and distribution. At the market level, this paper identifies a time margin of firm competition that is often hidden inside aggregate matching functions. Firms compete not only through wages, amenities, vacancy creation, and recruiting in- tensity, but also through how quickly they move applicants through the recruiting pipeline. The tightness gradients and the model’s recruiting-state shifter connect this within-vacancy margin to the cyclicality of matching efficiency. From an institutional-design perspective, the findings suggest that rules gov- erning application visibility and review timing can shape who receives consider- ation. Application windows, batching rules, timestamp visibility, recruiter dash- boards, and information about vacancy status can all affect whether queue po- sition translates into contact probability. The relevant challenge is not simply to make firms wait, but to reduce timing-driven consideration wedges while pre- serving firms’ ability to move quickly when waiting is costly. This distinction is especially important as recruiting technology changes.   Recent work shows that AI-powered tools can alter both worker search and employer screening (Le Bar- banchon, Hensvik and Rathelot, 2026; Wiles, Munyikwa and Horton, 2025; Aka et al., 2025).   My results suggest a complementary point: improved technology may compress particular stages of recruiting, but sequential timing remains a dis- tinct friction that can persist or reappear elsewhere in the hiring pipeline. The framework opens a broader research agenda. A first extension is to connect contact initiation to downstream hiring stages while allowing workers to choose both when and where to apply. A general-equilibrium model in which firms jointly choose wages, amenities, recruiting intensity, and contact speed would make tim- 47

ing an endogenous dimension of competition. Such a framework could study how market concentration and monopsony shape firms’ willingness to wait, how work- ers sort across vacancies with heterogeneous queues and initiation policies, and how these choices feed back into matching efficiency and inequality. The descrip- tive heterogeneity by firm prominence, offer competitiveness, and recruiting or- ganization documented in this paper motivates these questions, while leaving the equilibrium sorting mechanism for future work.   A complementary direction is to study how attention, recall, and information acquisition evolve within recruit- ing pipelines.   Firms may differ not only in when they initiate contact, but also in which applications they review, which previously observed candidates they re- call, and how contact changes the value of acquiring further information.   New technologies may alter each of these margins and reallocate timing frictions across stages of the hiring process. More broadly, the paper provides a framework for treating within-vacancy tim- ing as an endogenous component of matching. Standard search models often ab- stract from the sequence of evaluation, contact, interviews, and offers; the evidence here shows that this sequence shapes both who is considered and how market conditions translate into matches. Bringing empirically disciplined employer-side dynamics into search models offers a route to understanding not only how many matches are formed, but how workers are selected into them. A richer theory of matching should therefore explain not only whether workers and vacancies meet, but how the dynamics of employer decisions determine who ultimately matches. 48

B   Details of Machine Learning Predictions  This section discusses the contact propensity prediction model in detail. The pur- pose of the machine-learning (ML) component is to construct a resume-job contact propensity score that can be interpreted as an estimate of contact likelihood un- der a state-0 screening environment. This score is then used to quantify the fore- gone match quality from contacting early. To preserve a clean interpretation, the ML models exclude variables that directly encode the submission order and other timing-related variation. Instead, I build the model using only posting character- istics and resume attributes as predictors.  B.1   State-0 Environment and Feature Set  The ML models are designed to approximate contact propensity under an early- stage screening environment (“state-0”), before the recruiting process becomes en- dogenously shaped by prior contacts, time pressure, or other recruiting-state con- straints. Specifically, I train the models on the first application submitted within each posting. This design has two advantages: First, it anchors the estimated map- ping from resume and job features to contact outcomes to a common early point in the recruiting timeline and avoids implicitly conditioning the model on later- stage dynamics. Second, the model is able to predict how later submissions would be evaluated under the early-stage screening environment within the experiment, thereby creating a reasonable counterfactual to quantify the potential quality loss from rushing to contact.   This yields a training and evaluation sample of 11,114 submissions, one per posting. I use the following feature set as predictors:  •   Posting characteristics , including state, firm ID, experimental wave, submis- sion month, Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) code, three-digit Stan- dard Occupational Classification (SOC3) category, and a hiring intermediary platform indicator.  •   Resume headline attributes , including race, gender, age, education, political leaning indicators, and pronoun indicators.  •   Resume work-history details : number of prior positions listed, and for up to three prior positions: (i) job type (1,512 categories), (ii) job title (23 categories), and (iii) duration in months. 80

PAGE 82
Binary indicators are treated as numerical regressors.   For numerical covariates (including binaries), missing values are imputed using the training-fold median; for binaries this coincides with imputing the modal value in the common case where the median is 0.  Categorical representation   Since the job type of resume entries contains 1,512 categories, it can generate a large sparse matrix, which may lower the predictive performance of some algorithms. I therefore consider two encoding approaches to group rare categories and shrink the effective number of categories:  •   Frequency-based grouping.   I cap the number of distinct categories retained for high-cardinality type fields by keeping the   K   most frequent labels and mapping all other labels to an “Other” bucket.   Specifically, for the latest resume entry (the most recent job prior to application submission), I one-hot encode the type variable with a maximum of 300 categories using infrequent- category handling, and the other resume entries are pooled and mapped sim- ilarly.  •   Text-based clustering.   I also use an unsupervised text-based approach for encoding. I construct a term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) representation of the raw type labels and cluster the labels into 300 clusters using MiniBatch   k -means. Each type label is replaced by its cluster ID, then encoded analogously to the frequency-based approach (the most recent entry separately, all other entries pooled). This provides a data-driven grouping based on lexical similarity rather than frequency alone.  B.2   Candidate Models and Evaluation Metrics  I considered three “workhorse” model classes as initial benchmarks:  •   Elastic-net logistic regression : A linear model for binary outcomes (logis- tic regression) with combined L1/L2 regularization. It is fast and stable in sparse feature spaces, but relies on encoded inputs and can miss nonlineari- ties and complex interactions.  •   ExtraTrees classifier : An ensemble of heavily randomized decision trees that captures nonlinearities and interactions with minimal feature engineering, but its probability outputs may be less well-calibrated and less interpretable. 81

PAGE 83
•   CatBoost gradient boosting :   A gradient boosting algorithm designed for tabular problems, especially with many categorical predictors. It uses spe- cialized categorical handling, but is more complex, less transparent, and re- quires careful validation. I compare the three models using sparse encoded features to assess overall performance. Since CatBoost uses specialized categorical handling and boosting schemes, it handles high-dimensional categorical variables efficiently. I therefore also include a CatBoost model with the native, ungrouped categorical variables as predictors. It is attractive in this setting because it avoids high-dimensional one- hot expansions for high-cardinality categories, while still allowing flexible nonlin- earities and interactions via gradient boosting. Models are evaluated with prob- ability and ranking metrics computed on out-of-fold predictions for first applica- tions.  Out-of-fold procedure   A central design choice is to generate   out-of-fold (OOF)  predictions at the posting level. Let ̂   p ij   denote the out-of-fold cross-fit predicted probability of contact from this model.   Out-of-fold prediction ensures ̂   p ij   is not mechanically inflated by in-sample overfitting and vacancy-level leakage. I imple- ment 5-fold cross-fitting using GroupKFold: 1. Construct folds using only the first submission of each posting, with postings partitioned into 5 folds. 2. For each fold   f   , train the model on order- 1   applications from postings in the training folds. 3. Score applications in held-out postings. 4. Concatenate predictions across folds to calculate model performance statis- tics. C.5   Selective and Batch Review  The model used for estimation assumes that arrival and review coincide at the contact-initiation margin. This subsection discusses how the logic extends when firms choose which applications to review or review applications in batches.  Selective review.   Suppose arrived applications can be reviewed at cost   c R   ≥   0 , after which the firm observes screening value   v . For an unreviewed application with observable attributes, define a review index   ζ   by  E [( v   −   ζ ) +   |   observables ] =   c R .   (C.13) In a static set of arrived applications with independent values, Weitzman’s Pan- dora rule implies that the firm reviews applications in descending order of   ζ   and stops reviewing when the best revealed value exceeds the highest remaining index. F   Model-Guided Reduced-Form Heterogeneity  This appendix reports additional reduced-form heterogeneity in the daily early ap- plication advantage. In the model, the strength of the early application advantage depends on two broad forces: the effective arrival intensity of addi- tional applicant opportunities,   λ , and the shadow cost of delaying contact,   κ . The covariates studied here proxy for environments that may shift these forces, includ- ing applicant queue thickness, offer competitiveness, evaluation costs, and firm recruiting organization. F.1   Market Structure  Figure F.1 relates the daily early application advantage to local posting-market concentration.   Concentration is measured using Herfindahl-Hirschman indices constructed from Revelio Labs job posting data at the state and state-by-industry levels.   Higher concentration is associated with a smaller timing gradient, espe- cially in the more granular state-by-industry measures. One interpretation is that concentrated posting markets reduce the competitive pressure firms face to con- tact quickly, lowering the shadow cost of delay. This interpretation is consistent 118

PAGE 120
with work using posting data to measure local labor-market concentration (Azar, Marinescu and Steinbaum, 2022) and with recent evidence linking labor market concentration to firm hiring strategies (Dodini and Willén, 2025; Schubert et al., 2025). I treat the evidence as descriptive, since concentration may also be corre- lated with firm composition, job types, and local applicant behavior. Figure F.3 examines a related queue-thickness proxy: the firm’s share of post- ings in the relevant local market-month. I use posting share as a descriptive mea- sure of firm prominence in the local recruiting market. Prominent employers may face thicker applicant queues, increasing the option value of waiting and reducing the early application advantage.   Because posting-share coverage is incomplete, Figure F.2 compares the geographic and time-series coverage of the posting-share sample to the full experimental sample. The relationship between posting share and the timing gradient is negative outside the immediate post-pandemic recovery and close to zero during Waves 2-4, suggesting that the relevance of firm promi- nence varies with broader recruiting conditions.   The results are similar when posting shares are defined at the state-month and state-by-industry-month levels. These patterns are consistent with directed application behavior (Cai, Gautier and Wolthoff, 2025) and granular search models (Jarosch, Nimczik and Sorkin, 2024), but I interpret them only as descriptive evidence on queue thickness. Taken together, the market structure results suggest that concentration and firm prominence may operate through different margins. Concentration is more natu- rally linked to the shadow cost of delay, by changing the competitive pressure firms face when deciding how quickly to contact. Firm posting share is more nat- urally linked to effective arrival intensity, by proxying for the thickness of the ap- plicant queue facing prominent employers. Due to data limitations, I leave a fuller analysis of market structure and recruiting timing for future work. Offer competitiveness.   I construct posting-based measures of firms’ advertised offers relative to comparable entry-level postings in the same local market.   For each feature   x , the level gap is  G x f mt   = ¯ x mt   −   ¯ x f mt ,  where   ¯ x mt   and   ¯ x f mt   denote the local-market and firm averages.   For advertised salary and posting duration, the regressions use   log ¯ x mt   −   log ¯ x f mt   to reduce right skewness. For remote-friendliness, I use the market-minus-firm difference in post- ing shares. Positive salary and remote-friendliness gaps indicate less competitive advertised offers, while the posting-duration gap captures relative vacancy persis- tence. Comparable postings are restricted to O*NET Job Zones 1 and 2, and local markets follow the state-month or state-by-industry-month definition used in the corresponding specification.  Human-resource organization.   I use three firm-level measures:  •   1 { federal contractor } : an indicator for federal-contractor status.  •   Labor-law violations per employee   (std.) : the number of violations related to employment recorded since 2000 in the Good Jobs First Violation Tracker, divided by Compustat employment.  •   Callback centralization : following Kline, Rose and Walters (2022), the un- derlying centralization index is measured as the number of distinct phone numbers used by the firm to contact applicants divided by the total number of vacancies with at least one callback times -1. It is used without modifica- tion.  Financial characteristics.   Following Kline, Rose and Walters (2022), I use annual Compustat data averaged over 2010-2018:  •   Log employment   (std.) : the natural log of average employment.  •   Total assets per employee   (std.) : total assets divided by employment.  •   Gross profit per employee   (std.) : gross profit divided by employment.  •   Total revenue per employee   (std.) : total revenue divided by employment. Firms without Compustat coverage are omitted from specifications using these variables. 128
